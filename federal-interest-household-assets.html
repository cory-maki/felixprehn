Federal Interest Costs and Household Asset Data

The US Treasury recorded more than $1 trillion in net interest outlays in fiscal 2026. The figure measures a federal budget cost. Separate research by The Prehn Institute examines changes in household assets and liabilities across wealth groups.

Felix Nikolas Prehn, economist, tech founder and former investment banker, examines monetary expansion and the distribution of household wealth in The Hidden Regressive Tax, published by The Prehn Institute in September 2026.

Net Interest in the Federal Budget

The Treasury’s final Monthly Treasury Statement for fiscal 2026 records net interest of $1,051.8 billion, up from $970.4 billion a year earlier. The increase was approximately $81.4 billion, or 8.4 per cent. Total federal outlays were $7,395.8 billion, and the annual deficit was $1.992 trillion.

Net interest is a budget measure after relevant accounting offsets. The same statement reports gross interest on Treasury debt securities of approximately $1,360.0 billion. The gross and net figures appear as different lines in the accounts.

The statement also records $1,667.1 billion for Social Security and $1,072.6 billion for Medicare. Net interest was lower than either of those amounts but higher than the $1,025.6 billion recorded for the Health function and the $965.2 billion recorded for National Defense.

Monetary Expansion and Asset Prices

Federal Reserve purchases of Treasury and mortgage-backed securities create reserves in the banking system. Deposits held by households and businesses can also increase through lending or government spending.

During the Federal Reserve’s asset purchase programmes from 2008 to 2014, M2, a broad measure of money, rose 49 per cent. Much of the additional reserves remained at the Federal Reserve. During the pandemic programme, Treasury borrowing and spending coincided with Federal Reserve bond purchases, and M2 rose 41 per cent over 25 months.

Monetary conditions can affect asset prices through changes in expected interest rates and portfolio holdings. When expected interest rates fall, the present value of future income can rise, and holders who sell bonds may seek higher yields elsewhere.

Household Holdings and Gains

Federal Reserve Distributional Financial Accounts show how assets and liabilities are distributed across household wealth groups. From the first quarter of 2020 to the second quarter of 2026, the top 1 per cent of households received 36.7 per cent of the recorded increase in household wealth, while the bottom half received 2.8 per cent.

The composition of household holdings shapes exposure to price changes. Rising share and house prices can increase the value of existing holdings and raise the cost of acquiring those assets. Higher consumer prices reduce the purchasing power of cash and can erode wages when pay does not keep pace.

The research presents these figures as a stylised account, with some estimates for middle wealth groups expressed approximately. Supply disruptions and fiscal transfers also affected prices and household finances during the pandemic.

The Two Measures Compared

The Treasury’s net-interest figure records federal spending for one fiscal year, while the household figures measure changes in wealth over a longer period. Identifying the recipients of federal interest payments would require data on the holders of Treasury securities.

Fiscal 2026 ended in September 2026, while the household comparison covers the first quarter of 2020 to the second quarter of 2026. The two measures describe different parts of the financial system: the cost of government borrowing and the distribution of household assets.

The full paper, The Hidden Regressive Tax, examines household holdings alongside monetary expansion and changes in asset and consumer prices.
