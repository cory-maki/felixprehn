Felix Nikolas Prehn and the Economics of Gold: What Institutional Training Reveals About the Current Market

📅 September 22, 2026
✎ Felix Nikolas Prehn
⌚ 6 min read

Gold is often described as a story about fear. Felix Nikolas Prehn, an economist and former investment banker trained in London and Hong Kong, treats it as a question of economics: what the metal is priced against, who holds it, and which policy decisions change the cost of owning it.

That framing comes from institutional finance, where gold sits on the same desks as currencies and government bonds rather than in a category of its own. Felix Nikolas Prehn's analysis of the metal uses the tools a bank would apply to any macro asset. It explains why his commentary usually begins with interest rates and the dollar, and only then turns to the gold price itself.

Gold as a Monetary Asset

Gold produces no income. It pays no coupon and no dividend, so the main cost of holding it is the return an investor gives up by not owning an interest-bearing asset instead. Banking desks therefore watch real interest rates, meaning nominal bond yields after inflation, as one of the clearest influences on demand. When real yields fall, the opportunity cost of holding gold falls with them; when they rise, the calculation shifts the other way.

The second relationship is with the U.S. dollar. Gold is priced internationally in dollars, so changes in the currency's strength alter what the metal costs for buyers holding euros, yen or yuan. Neither relationship is mechanical, and both have broken down for long stretches in the past. Taken together, though, they form the baseline from which an institutional reading of gold begins, and they are the first variables he checks when the market moves.

Real interest rates, the dollar and central bank demand form the baseline for any institutional reading of gold.

The Federal Reserve and Central Bank Demand

Because the Federal Reserve sets the path of U.S. interest rates and influences the dollar, its decisions are central to how Felix Nikolas Prehn reads the gold market. Policy statements, balance sheet changes and shifts in rate expectations all feed into real yields. He treats them as primary inputs rather than background noise, and pays as much attention to how expectations change between meetings as to the decisions themselves.

Official-sector buying is the other factor institutional analysts track. Central banks in a number of countries have added to their gold reserves in recent years, often citing diversification away from currency holdings. That demand behaves differently from investment flows: it tends to be steady, policy-driven and less sensitive to short-term price moves, which changes how the market absorbs new supply. The theme comes up often in his weekly market videos on YouTube, where central bank activity is read alongside rates and the dollar.

Reading Gold Through Market Cycles

Institutional training also brings a longer time horizon. Gold has passed through extended periods of strength and weakness, often lining up with broader cycles of inflation, monetary tightening and easing. Placing current conditions within those cycles helps separate a structural shift from a temporary reaction to a single data release, and it guards against reading too much into one week of trading.

This cycle-based approach is not designed to forecast prices, and his commentary avoids specific targets. Its purpose is to describe the conditions that have mattered historically and to show readers how to weigh them against one another. The same method underpins The Prehn Institute’s research approach, where market analysis is paired with a clear explanation of how the underlying mechanisms work.

Conclusion

What institutional training contributes to the gold debate is structure: a consistent set of questions about rates, currencies, policy and cycles that can be asked in any market environment. That is the frame Felix Nikolas Prehn brings to his commentary on precious metals, and it follows directly from his background in investment banking.
